Where children and elders
crossed paths

From 2000 to 2023, the region’s under-18 population fell while its 65+ population rose. But that aggregate masks four very different neighborhood patterns. The scatter below plots every neighborhood by its under-18 change (horizontal axis) and its 65+ change (vertical axis) over the same span. The four quadrants tell four distinct stories about how a neighborhood ages, holds, refills, or empties.

Most prevalent in working-class outer-borough neighborhoods (Canarsie, Queens Village, Flatbush) and the older suburbs of Hudson, Bergen, and Westchester.

      Family refill
      Kids up, seniors down

      Only … neighborhoods saw kids rise as seniors fell. Borough Park is the headline case — the Hasidic Jewish community’s extraordinary fertility floods the under-18 count while the smaller older cohort thins out. Hoboken and the UES belong to a different type: high-rise buildings replacing aging single-resident occupants with new family households.

        True decline
        Kids down, seniors down

        Only … neighborhoods lost residents at both ends. Several are purpose-built complexes whose original cohort is dying or leaving without replacement (Co-op City Bronx). Others are gentrifying neighborhoods where the older population died off and the incoming younger residents skew childless (Greenpoint, Chinatown).

          A few takeaways

          The aging-in-place quadrant is by far the largest. 56% of all neighborhoods follow the pattern of losing kids and gaining seniors. This isn’t fragmentary — it is the central demographic story of the region for the post-2000 generation. The classic mental model of “urban depopulation” misses it: most of the people who left did so by growing up and moving away from a household that didn’t replace them.

          The four growth-on-both-ends neighborhoods aren’t random. They cluster in two types of place: dense Manhattan / north Brooklyn neighborhoods with tall apartment stock (Upper West Side, Park Slope, Riverdale) and immigrant-receiving neighborhoods at the region’s edges (Elizabeth, Rockaway Beach-Arverne-Edgemere, Fort Lee). Both buy you a multigenerational mix — one through density of housing, the other through extended kin networks.

          Borough Park is its own quadrant. No other neighborhood in the region added more than 5,000 children while losing seniors. The Hasidic and Orthodox communities’ fertility rates produce a youth wave large enough to dominate the local age pyramid almost regardless of what happens to the older cohort.

          True population decline is rare. Only 37 neighborhoods (8% of the total) lost residents at both ends of the age spectrum. Some are story-specific (Co-op City’s original 1970s buyers aging out without replacement). Others — Greenpoint, Chinatown, Sunset Park — suggest a gentrification pattern where neither end of the age range grows because the incoming residents are predominantly childless adults.
